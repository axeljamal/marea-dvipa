# Crafting pattern aesthetics

The aim is never only "pretty". It is beautiful, pleasing, unique and meaningful, with every choice explainable. Use this skill whenever designing, developing or reviewing a pattern, motif set, colourway or layout, and in daily development ideas. It pairs with heritage-signature-design (origins and credit) and the pattern tools.

## The loop
1. **Intention.** Write one sentence: who it is for, what it should feel like, where it will live (product, scale, viewing distance), and what it must honour.
2. **Gather widely.** Pull at least three references from different fields: one from the source culture, one from another visual tradition, and one from outside visual art (music, architecture, botany, mathematics, cartography, dance, film). Note what each teaches about structure, rhythm or colour, not just how it looks.
3. **Diverge.** Make several distinct options before judging. Use the creative moves below.
4. **Converge.** Critique with the lenses and tests below. Keep what survives and name why.
5. **Refine.** Change one thing at a time and compare side by side with the previous version and the source.
6. **Originality check.** Ask what is unmistakably ours and what could be mistaken for an existing work. Strengthen the first and remove the second.
7. **Document.** Record the decisions, reasoning and references next to the work (spec, ORIGINS, design journal) so others can learn from it and credit it.

## Creative moves (divergent thinking)
- **Transpose a structure across disciplines.** Turn a rhythm into a repeat (gamelan gong cycles as row accents; musical meter and syncopation as regular and offset placements). Turn a growth law into a layout (phyllotaxis, the golden angle). Turn a map, a weave draft or an architectural plan into a grid.
- **Put two traditions in conversation.** Let each keep its voice: one supplies the structure, the other the motifs or colour (e.g. Bomba borders framing Balinese heroes).
- **Constrain to invent.** Two inks only; one motif at five scales; no straight lines; a single gesture repeated. Constraints force a personal solution.
- **Change the scale of attention.** Zoom a detail into a field (a petal vein becomes an isen fill); shrink a hero into texture.
- **Add a hand to a system.** Let a rule set the order, then add controlled irregularity: wobble, seeded variation, articulation (Vera Molnár's controlled disorder).
- **SCAMPER:** substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to another use, eliminate, reverse.

## Critical lenses (convergent thinking)
- **Order with surprise.** We enjoy regularity, and we notice where it breaks (Gombrich, *The Sense of Order*). Give every pattern a clear underlying order plus a few deliberate breaks or accents.
- **Optimal complexity.** Pleasure tends to peak at moderate complexity and novelty, not at the extremes (Berlyne's arousal-potential research). Easy-to-process images also feel pleasing (processing fluency: Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, 2004). Aim for patterns that read at a glance and reward a closer look.
- **Living structure.** Christopher Alexander's 15 properties (*The Nature of Order*): levels of scale, strong centres, boundaries, alternating repetition, positive space, good shape, local symmetries, deep interlock and ambiguity, contrast, gradients, roughness, echoes, the void, simplicity and inner calm, and not-separateness. Check which are present and which are missing.
- **Grammar of ornament.** Owen Jones (1856): ornament should grow from the construction; flowing lines should spring from a parent stem; natural forms should be conventionalised rather than copied. Check that motifs belong to the structure rather than being sprinkled on it.
- **Gestalt.** Proximity, similarity, continuity, closure and figure-ground decide what the eye groups. Make sure the groups it finds are the ones you meant.
- **Colour is relative.** Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color*: the same ink changes with its neighbours. Johannes Itten's seven contrasts (hue, light-dark, cold-warm, complementary, simultaneous, saturation, extension): pick one or two to lead. Get the value structure right first, then hue.
- **Space.** The Japanese idea of *ma*: intervals are part of the design. Ground is a shape, not leftovers.
- **Proportion.** Use Fibonacci numbers and the golden ratio as tools for growth and proportion, not as proof of beauty. Evidence for a universal golden-ratio preference is weak, so judge by eye and by purpose.
- **Meaning and respect.** Does each heritage element sit where its tradition would honour it? Is anything sacred being trivialised (placement on floors or feet, cut through, used as filler)? Are the origins credited?

## Tests to run every time
1. **Squint or blur:** does the value structure hold, and is there one clear first read?
2. **Thumbnail and distance:** view at 10% size and at product viewing distance. Is it still legible and pleasing?
3. **Tile:** repeat it 3x3 or more and look for seams, unintended stripes or diagonals (tramlines), clumps and holes.
4. **One-colour:** print it in a single ink. Does the drawing still stand without colour?
5. **Ground swap:** try it on light, dark and mid grounds; check ink contrast.
6. **Scale at use:** see it at the real size on the real product (scarf, shirt, wall) before approving.
7. **Why this?:** every element must answer what it adds. Remove anything that cannot.
8. **Originality:** name the three things that make it ours, and check it does not reproduce a specific living maker's design or a trademark.

## How to present work
- Show options side by side with the source or reference.
- For each option give one line of intention, the lenses it satisfies, the risk, and the references (with links where possible).
- Recommend one, say why, and say what you would try next.
